Pain that starts suddenly, often at night, in the big toe. The joint becomes **swollen, red and warm**, and the pain can wake you up. That's how **gout** usually shows up — a type of arthritis that, according to the CDC, **usually affects one joint at a time**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

The same study found that **only a third (33%)** of people with gout were taking treatment to lower uric acid.<sup>[3]</sup>

## Why it happens

Gout happens when **uric acid** (urate) builds up in the body. Urate forms when **purines** break down; purines are found in body tissues and many foods. Normally it leaves the body in urine, but if the body makes too much or removes too little, it builds up and forms **needle-shaped crystals** in the joints, causing inflammation.<sup>[2]</sup>

That said, **many people with high uric acid levels never develop gout**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Flares and complications

Gout flares usually last **a week or two**, and symptoms disappear in between.<sup>[2]</sup> Without treatment, flares can become **more frequent and last longer**.<sup>[1]</sup> Other complications include:<sup>[2]</sup>

- **Tophi:** buildups of crystals that form hard lumps under the skin and can damage bones and joints.
- **Kidney stones**, from high uric acid levels.

## How it is treated

NIAMS sums it up: with **early diagnosis, treatment and lifestyle changes**, gout is **one of the most controllable forms of arthritis**.<sup>[2]</sup> The American College of Rheumatology's 2020 guideline strongly recommends:<sup>[4]</sup>

- **During a flare:** colchicine, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or corticosteroids.
- **Urate-lowering therapy** for people with tophi, joint damage visible on X-rays, or frequent flares, with **allopurinol as the first choice**.
- A **"treat-to-target"** strategy: adjusting the dose with regular blood tests until urate stays **below 6 mg/dL**.
- When starting that therapy, adding **anti-inflammatory preventive medication for at least 3 to 6 months**.

> No medication should be taken without medical advice: each one has contraindications and side effects.

## Habits that help

The CDC recommends:<sup>[1]</sup>

1. Getting regular **physical activity**, at least **150 minutes a week** at moderate intensity.
2. **Limiting purine-rich foods and drinks**, especially beer and hard liquor.

> If you have sudden attacks of pain in a joint, an assessment can confirm whether it's gout and help you keep it under control so it doesn't come back.
