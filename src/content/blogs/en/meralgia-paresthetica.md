**Meralgia paresthetica** is a condition that causes **burning, aching, tingling, stabbing or numbness in the thigh**.<sup>[1]</sup> It happens when the **lateral femoral cutaneous nerve** is compressed. This nerve only carries **sensation** from the skin on the **front and outer side of the thigh**.<sup>[1]</sup> It leaves the spine, travels through the pelvis and down the thigh, and it usually gets trapped as it passes through the **groin**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

It can be very bothersome, but it is **not dangerous** to your health.<sup>[1]</sup> Researchers estimate it affects **3 to 4 out of every 10,000 people each year**, and although it is relatively common, it is **frequently misdiagnosed**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Signs to watch for

- **Burning, tingling, "pins and needles" or numbness** on the front or outer thigh<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- Almost always on **one side** of the body<sup>[1]</sup>
- Pain that may spread to the **outer side of the knee**<sup>[1]</sup>
- **Extra sensitivity to touch**: even the brush of clothing can hurt<sup>[1]</sup>
- Symptoms that **get worse after walking or standing for a long time** and often ease when you lie down<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

Because this nerve does not control any muscles, meralgia paresthetica **does not weaken the leg** or affect how you move it.<sup>[1]</sup> Early on, symptoms are usually mild and come and go; in advanced cases a shooting pain can appear that does not change with position.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Why it happens and who is at higher risk

The nerve can be compressed by **pressure, swelling or an injury** in the area.<sup>[1]</sup> Well-known risk factors include:

## How it is diagnosed

A **medical history and physical exam** are usually enough: the doctor reviews your symptoms, tests your response to light touch and your reflexes, and may do a **pelvic compression test**.<sup>[1]</sup> A key clue is that the loss of feeling stays within the area of the thigh served by this nerve.<sup>[2]</sup>

If the cause is unclear, you may need **blood tests** (thyroid, B vitamins, lead, blood sugar and hemoglobin), an **X-ray** of the pelvis and thigh to rule out other problems, or a **CT scan or MRI** to check the spine, for example if a herniated disc is suspected.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Treatment options

Most cases improve with **simple measures**, and the first step is treating the cause.<sup>[1]</sup>

If surgery is done, the nerve can be **freed up** (decompression) or **cut** (neurectomy); the latter relieves pain more reliably but leaves **permanent numbness** in the area of the thigh the nerve supplies.<sup>[2]</sup> Physical therapy may help, although research on how well it works is limited.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Outlook

The outlook is usually **good**: about **85%** of people recover with conservative treatment.<sup>[1]</sup> When the cause is surgery or a direct nerve injury, it typically improves within **about three months**, and when it is related to pregnancy, it typically improves **after delivery**.<sup>[1]</sup> Symptoms often go away on their own, but **they commonly come back**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What you can do

It cannot always be prevented, but you can lower your risk by keeping a **healthy weight**, wearing **loose clothing** and avoiding girdles and tight belts, including **tool belts**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you have burning or numbness in your thigh that is not getting better, if treatment is not working or causes side effects, or if the symptoms affect your quality of life.<sup>[1]</sup> Without treatment, the pain, burning or numbness can increase and make it hard to walk or move normally.<sup>[1]</sup> Because other problems, such as a herniated disc, can cause similar symptoms, an assessment helps confirm where the pain is coming from.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If you notice burning, tingling or numbness on the outer thigh, especially when walking or standing, an assessment can confirm whether a pinched nerve is to blame and point you to the right treatment.
