Despite the name, **you don't have to play tennis** to get it. Tennis elbow causes **pain on the outside of the elbow** and, according to the NHS, it is most common between the **ages of 35 and 54**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Why it happens

The NHS links it to **inflamed tendons**, the cords that connect muscle to bone, from **repetitive gripping or twisting of the wrist and forearm**. Some examples:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Computer work
- Manual tasks such as sewing or using a screwdriver
- Leisure activities such as tennis or playing an instrument

## Symptoms

- **Pain on the outside of the elbow**, which may be worse when lifting or gripping objects, or moving the wrist<sup>[1]</sup>
- Tenderness or swelling at the elbow<sup>[1]</sup>
- Pain in the forearm<sup>[1]</sup>
- Difficulty fully straightening the arm<sup>[1]</sup>

## How long it lasts

It usually **gets better after resting the arm for a few weeks**, although it can sometimes **last more than a year**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## What to do at home

The NHS recommends:<sup>[1]</sup>

- **Avoiding or reducing** activities that make symptoms worse.
- Applying a **hot or cold pack** wrapped in a towel, for up to 20 minutes every 2 to 3 hours.
- Doing **simple exercises** that bend and straighten the arm.
- Using acetaminophen or an **anti-inflammatory gel** for pain.
- Considering a **forearm strap** or a wrist or elbow brace from a pharmacy.

If you use ibuprofen gel, **don't smoke or go near flames**: it is flammable and can cause severe burns.<sup>[1]</sup>

## What about steroid injections?

The authors concluded that the corticosteroid injection **led to worse clinical outcomes after 1 year** than placebo, with **more recurrences**. Physiotherapy made no significant difference at 1 year, although, combined with the placebo injection, it did improve results at 4 weeks.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What the overall evidence says

A 2015 meta-analysis of **22 clinical trials with 2,280 patients** compared different non-surgical treatments (injections, physiotherapy, shock wave therapy, laser, ultrasound and others) with observation or placebo. It concluded that they **don't provide a clear clinical benefit in the intermediate to long term** compared with observation alone.<sup>[3]</sup>

What does that mean in practice? Tennis elbow **tends to improve over time**, and the goal of treatment is to **ease the pain while it heals**, without risking a worse outcome.

## When to see a doctor

According to the NHS:<sup>[1]</sup>

- See a doctor if you **still have pain after at least 2 weeks** of rest and home care.
- **Physiotherapy** may be considered if symptoms persist after **6 weeks**.
- **Surgery** is an option if symptoms persist after **6 to 12 months**.

> If elbow pain isn't improving or keeps you from working, an assessment can help you choose the treatment that's right for you, with clear information about what works and what doesn't.
