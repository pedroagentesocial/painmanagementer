**Cubital tunnel syndrome**, also called **ulnar nerve entrapment**, happens when the **ulnar nerve** is irritated or squeezed on the **inside of the elbow**.<sup>[1]</sup> There the nerve runs through a narrow passage, the **cubital tunnel**, right under the **medial epicondyle**, the bony bump we all know as the **"funny bone."**<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Very little tissue protects the nerve at that spot, which is why bumping it sends that familiar jolt down your arm.<sup>[1]</sup>

The ulnar nerve gives feeling to the **little finger and half of the ring finger**, and it controls many of the small muscles of the hand and some of the forearm muscles you use to grip.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> That is why, when it is compressed at the elbow, most of the symptoms show up **in the hand**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Signs to watch for

- **Numbness and tingling in the ring and little fingers**, which often come and go<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- Symptoms that **get worse with the elbow bent**: while driving, holding a phone or sleeping<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- **Waking up at night** with numb fingers<sup>[2]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>
- **Pain on the inside of the elbow**<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- In more advanced cases, a **weaker grip** and **clumsy fingers**<sup>[2]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>

A simple way to tell it apart from **carpal tunnel syndrome**: cubital tunnel affects the **little and ring fingers**, while carpal tunnel affects the **thumb, index and middle fingers**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Why it happens

Often no exact cause is found.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Bending the elbow stretches the nerve and reduces its blood supply, which is why symptoms build up with the arm bent.<sup>[2]</sup> Things that can contribute include:

- Keeping the **elbow bent for long periods**, including during sleep<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- **Leaning on the elbow**, for example on an armrest or a desk<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- The nerve **sliding and "snapping"** over the epicondyle again and again as you move<sup>[1]</sup>
- A **past elbow fracture or dislocation**, **arthritis**, **bone spurs**, **cysts** or **swelling** of the joint<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- Activities that bend the elbow repeatedly, such as **baseball pitching**<sup>[2]</sup>

MedlinePlus adds other factors tied to ulnar nerve problems, such as **diabetes** and **smoking**.<sup>[3]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

The doctor asks about your symptoms and activities and examines your elbow, your hand and also your **neck**, because a problem in the cervical spine can cause similar symptoms.<sup>[2]</sup> They may tap over the nerve at the funny bone (**Tinel's sign**), check whether the nerve slips out of place when you bend your elbow, and test the strength and feeling in your fingers.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Treatment options

Treatment almost always starts **without surgery**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

**Steroid injections into the elbow** are generally **avoided** because of the risk of injuring the nerve.<sup>[2]</sup>

If surgery is needed, the main options are **cubital tunnel release**, in which the ligament that forms the "roof" of the tunnel is cut to give the nerve more room; **anterior transposition of the ulnar nerve**, which moves it from behind the epicondyle to the front; and, less often, **medial epicondylectomy**, which removes part of that bony bump.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> These are usually done as outpatient procedures.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## Recovery

After surgery a splint is usually worn for a few days to a few weeks, and physical therapy is sometimes needed.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Nerves heal more slowly than other tissues, so full recovery **can take many months**.<sup>[1]</sup> Results are generally good, although some symptoms may remain if the compression was severe or there was already muscle loss.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What you can do

- **Don't lean on your elbow** or rest it on an armrest<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- **Sleep with your elbow straight**; a night splint or padded brace can help<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- **Take breaks** if your work or sport keeps your elbow bent for long stretches<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- If you wear a splint or cast, **make sure it isn't too tight**<sup>[3]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if your symptoms are **severe or last more than 6 weeks**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> When compression goes on for a long time, the hand muscles can **weaken and waste away**, and that muscle loss **cannot always be reversed**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> You should also get checked if, after an arm injury, you notice numbness, tingling, pain or weakness along the forearm and in the ring and little fingers.<sup>[3]</sup>

> If your little and ring fingers go numb, the inside of your elbow hurts or you wake up at night with a numb hand, an assessment can help find where the nerve is being squeezed and start treatment before you lose strength in your hand.
