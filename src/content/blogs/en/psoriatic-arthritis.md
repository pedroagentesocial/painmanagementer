**Psoriatic arthritis** is an inflammatory disease in which an overactive immune system attacks the **joints** and the **entheses**, the spots where tendons and ligaments attach to bone.<sup>[1]</sup> It is closely tied to **psoriasis**, the skin condition that causes scaly, inflamed patches: about **1 in 3 people with psoriasis** also develop psoriatic arthritis.<sup>[3]</sup> There is no cure, but treatment can **slow it down, ease the pain and protect the joints**, and early care leads to better results.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## Who gets it

Anyone can, but it is most common in adults and affects men and women equally.<sup>[1]</sup> Most people already have psoriasis, and the arthritis usually shows up **about 7 to 10 years after the skin symptoms** begin; a small group has joint pain before the rash appears.<sup>[1]</sup> Joint symptoms often start **between ages 30 and 50**.<sup>[3]</sup>

Genes play a part: about **4 in 10** people with psoriatic arthritis have a relative with the condition.<sup>[3]</sup> Obesity and severe psoriasis seem to raise the risk, and infections, injuries to a joint or bone, and stressful events may trigger it.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Signs to watch for

- **Painful, stiff and swollen joints**, with stiffness that is often worse in the morning or after resting<sup>[1]</sup>
- A **whole finger or toe swollen** like a sausage<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>
- Pain where tendons attach to bone, such as the **back of the heel** (Achilles tendon) or the **sole of the foot**<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>
- **Nail changes**: small dents (pitting), crumbling, discoloration or the nail lifting off the nail bed<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>
- Stiffness in the **neck, low back and hips** when the spine is involved<sup>[1]</sup>
- **Fatigue**<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>
- **Eye pain, redness and blurred vision** (uveitis), which needs prompt treatment to protect your vision<sup>[1]</sup>

Symptoms tend to come and go in **flares**, and how bad they are can change from one flare to the next.<sup>[3]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

There is no single test that confirms it.<sup>[2]</sup><sup>[3]</sup> The provider asks about your symptoms and any family history of psoriasis or psoriatic arthritis, and checks for swollen or tender joints, skin patches and nail changes.<sup>[2]</sup> **Blood tests** help rule out other conditions, such as rheumatoid arthritis, and **X-rays, ultrasound or MRI** can show changes in the joints, the entheses or the spine.<sup>[2]</sup>

It can take time to find the right medicine or combination, and the plan depends on which areas are affected and how severe they are.<sup>[2]</sup> The care is often shared between a **rheumatologist**, a **dermatologist**, therapists and your primary care provider.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What you can do

NIAMS recommends **quitting smoking**, since some studies link it to worse symptoms; keeping a **healthy weight**, which eases the load on your joints and may help your medicines work better; and **low-impact exercise** such as walking, cycling, swimming, yoga or tai chi, after checking with your doctor.<sup>[2]</sup> Heat or cold can ease sore joints.<sup>[3]</sup> Because living with a long-term condition can be hard, support groups or counseling can also help.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

If you have psoriasis and notice joint pain, morning stiffness, a swollen finger or toe, heel pain or nail changes, tell your doctor: **early care improves the outlook**.<sup>[1]</sup> Get prompt care for **eye pain, redness or blurred vision**.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If you live with psoriasis and your joints, heels or back hurt and feel stiff in the morning, an assessment can help tell inflammatory pain from wear and tear and connect you with the right treatment and specialists.
