Many people grow up hearing that period pain is just something you put up with. But when the pain **stops you from doing your normal activities**, or your pelvis hurts even outside your period, it may be **endometriosis**: a condition in which tissue similar to the lining of the womb grows **outside the womb**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

It is most often found in the pelvis, on the ovaries, the fallopian tubes and the pelvic lining, and sometimes on the bladder or bowel. Less often it appears outside the pelvis, for example in the chest.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> It can also affect transgender men and non-binary people who menstruate.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Symptoms

- **Severe period pain** that stops you from doing your normal activities<sup>[2]</sup>
- **Heavy periods**, needing to change pads or tampons every 1 to 2 hours, or bleeding through your clothes<sup>[2]</sup>
- **Pain in the lower belly and back** (pelvic area), even outside your period<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- Pain when you poo or pee, and pain during or after sex<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- **Extreme tiredness** (fatigue)<sup>[2]</sup>
- Bloating and nausea<sup>[1]</sup>
- Difficulty getting pregnant, low mood or anxiety<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

Symptoms vary a lot from person to person, and some people have none.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Why diagnosis takes so long

The WHO explains that the symptoms are varied and broad, so health workers may not recognize them easily, and many people don't know that chronic pelvic pain **is not normal**.<sup>[1]</sup> The NHS notes that the symptoms overlap with other conditions, such as adenomyosis, fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease and irritable bowel syndrome.<sup>[2]</sup>

The process usually starts with your symptoms and your menstrual history, and may include a physical exam and an **ultrasound** or **MRI**. A **laparoscopy** (keyhole surgery with a camera) can confirm the diagnosis, and sometimes areas of endometriosis can be removed during the same procedure.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## Treatment

<tr><td><strong>Hormonal treatment</strong></td><td>Combined contraceptives, progestins or GnRH analogues, among others; not used in people who are trying to get pregnant</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Surgery</strong></td><td>Removes lesions, adhesions and scar tissue when other treatments haven't worked; in some cases a hysterectomy is considered, but it is not a cure</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Multidisciplinary care</strong></td><td>Physical therapy and cognitive behavioral therapy can reduce pain and improve quality of life; support for long-term pain, fatigue and mental health</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Fertility treatment</strong></td><td>Ovulation induction, intrauterine insemination or IVF, depending on the case</td></tr>

The NHS warns that symptoms **can come back** after surgery, and that surgery can cause **adhesions** (scarring that makes organs stick together), so more than one operation may be needed. Symptoms usually stop after menopause, although they can sometimes continue.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if you think you may have endometriosis, if your symptoms are affecting your daily life, work or relationships, or if they don't improve with treatment or are getting worse.<sup>[2]</sup>

> If pelvic pain has been part of your life for years, you don't have to accept it as normal. A pain assessment can help you put together a plan to control it alongside your gynecologist.
