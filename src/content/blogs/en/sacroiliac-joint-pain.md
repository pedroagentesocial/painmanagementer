The **sacroiliac (SI) joints** join the **sacrum**, the triangle-shaped bone at the bottom of the spine, to the **ilium**, the top part of the pelvis. They are among the largest joints in the body and are used every time you move or shift your hips.<sup>[1]</sup> When one or both of them become inflamed, the condition is called **sacroiliitis**. Exact numbers are hard to pin down, but some studies estimate that **about one in four people with low back pain** have sacroiliitis.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Symptoms

The most common symptom is **low back pain**, which may:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Get worse after **sitting or standing in one position** for a long time
- Get worse when **turning or rotating the hips**
- Feel sharp and stabbing, or like a constant dull ache
- Spread to the **buttocks, hips or thighs**

Some people also have **morning stiffness that lasts more than an hour** after waking up.<sup>[1]</sup> Pain that goes on can disrupt sleep, reduce mobility and contribute to depression.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

A provider asks when the pain started and what makes it worse, then examines the back, hips and legs, tests range of motion and presses on the joints to find the movements that cause pain. **Pelvic X-rays, CT or MRI** can look at the joints, and **blood tests** help rule out infection and other inflammatory causes.<sup>[1]</sup> An **SI joint injection** with a local anesthetic can also be used as a diagnostic test: it is done the same way as a treatment injection.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Treatment options

Many cases can be managed **without surgery**, and treatment often combines several approaches.<sup>[2]</sup>

When sacroiliitis is caused by an inflammatory disease, a **rheumatologist** may be part of the care team.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

Cleveland Clinic recommends seeing a provider if you have **new or worsening low back pain**, if the pain **doesn't improve on its own within a week**, or if symptoms come back, get worse or stop responding to treatment.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If pain low in your back and buttock keeps returning when you sit, stand or turn, an assessment can find out whether the sacroiliac joint is the source and which treatment fits your case.
