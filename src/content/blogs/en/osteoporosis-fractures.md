Osteoporosis doesn't hurt... until a bone breaks. That's why the NIH call it a **"silent" disease**: it usually **causes no symptoms until there is a fracture**.<sup>[2]</sup> It develops when **bone mineral density and bone mass decrease**, or bone structure changes, and the bones become weaker.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Where bones break

Fractures can happen in any bone, but they are most common in the **hip, the vertebrae of the spine and the wrist**.<sup>[2]</sup>

### Spine fractures and back pain

A vertebral fracture can cause **severe back pain, loss of height** and changes in the spine such as a **hunched posture (kyphosis)**.<sup>[2]</sup> If you notice you've lost height or have new, severe back pain, especially after age 50, mention it to your doctor.

## Risk factors

According to NIAMS:<sup>[2]</sup>

- Being **female** and **age**
- **Small body size** and **family history**
- **Hormone changes**, such as menopause
- **Low calcium or vitamin D** intake
- Some **medical conditions and medications**
- **Inactivity**, heavy **alcohol** use and **smoking**

## How to protect your bones

NIAMS recommends:<sup>[2]</sup>

1. Eating a **diet rich in calcium and vitamin D**.
2. Staying **active** with **weight-bearing exercise**, such as walking.
3. **Taking your medication** if prescribed: it can help prevent fractures in people with osteoporosis.

Low physical fitness **raises the risk of falls and fractures**, so staying active protects you too.<sup>[2]</sup> Your doctor may recommend a **bone density test** based on your age and risk factors.

> If you have new back pain after age 50, or risk factors for osteoporosis, an assessment can help rule out fractures and protect your bone health.
