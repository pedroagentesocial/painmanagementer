After an amputation, many people still feel the missing arm or leg: it itches, it feels cold, it seems to be in an odd position. When those sensations become painful, it is called **phantom limb pain**. Cleveland Clinic stresses that the pain is **real, not imagined**, even though the body part is no longer there.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How common it is and when it starts

**More than half** of people who lose a limb have phantom pain.<sup>[1]</sup> It can begin as soon as the anesthetic wears off after surgery or take weeks to appear.<sup>[2]</sup> Many people notice it in the **first six months**, and after that its intensity and frequency usually go down, although research cited by Cleveland Clinic shows that most people still have some phantom pain **two years** after the amputation.<sup>[1]</sup> Each episode can last seconds, minutes, hours or days.<sup>[1]</sup>

The Royal Orthopaedic Hospital, an NHS trust, explains that feeling sensations in a missing limb is **normal** and can even happen in people who were born without a limb. Those sensations often fade over time, but they can last for years.<sup>[2]</sup>

Telling them apart matters, because stump pain can have a cause that needs its own treatment.<sup>[1]</sup>

## What it feels like

People describe phantom pain as **burning, stabbing, cramping, throbbing, tingling, pins and needles** or twisting.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> The limb may feel stuck in one position, move on its own, or feel bigger or smaller than it was.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## Why it happens

The exact cause isn't known. The leading explanation is a **miscommunication in the nervous system**: after the amputation, nerves can send excess or mixed signals, and the brain reorganizes the "map" it used for that limb and may misread those signals as pain.<sup>[1]</sup> The Royal Orthopaedic Hospital notes that the answer probably lies largely in the brain, which would explain why people born without a limb can also feel it.<sup>[2]</sup>

Phantom pain is more common after amputations of the **arm or fingers**, although it can affect any part of the body.<sup>[1]</sup> Having pain before and after surgery increases the risk.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Common triggers

Cleveland Clinic lists **fatigue, stress, anxiety, depression, infection, poor blood flow** and pressure or swelling on the stump as things that can start or worsen the pain. It may also be worse when someone who regularly uses a prosthesis isn't wearing it.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Treatment options

There is usually no single fix, and treatment often combines several approaches.<sup>[1]</sup>

Cleveland Clinic describes mirror therapy as about **20 minutes a day**, usually guided by a physical therapist.<sup>[1]</sup> The Royal Orthopaedic Hospital adds that it works best when the reflected limb lines up with where the phantom limb is felt, and that the experience can be emotional.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Can it be prevented?

There is no known way to prevent phantom limb pain. Some studies suggest that combining spinal and general anesthesia during the amputation may lower the risk.<sup>[1]</sup> The Royal Orthopaedic Hospital recommends working closely with your care team to control pain **before and after surgery**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

Talk to your doctor if phantom pain **interferes with your sleep or daily life**, or if you notice signs of infection in the residual limb, such as fever or changes in skin color.<sup>[1]</sup> Because stress, anxiety and depression can trigger the pain, a psychological evaluation may also be part of the assessment.<sup>[1]</sup>

> Phantom pain is real pain with real treatments. If it keeps you up at night or limits your day, an assessment can help you find the right combination of options.
