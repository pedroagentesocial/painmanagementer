A **bunion** is a bony bump on the inside of the foot, at the joint at the base of the big toe. It is linked to **hallux valgus**, a condition in which the big toe gradually drifts toward the smaller toes.<sup>[2]</sup> It usually develops slowly, and the pain tends to be **worse when you wear shoes and walk**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## How a bunion forms

Bunions develop at the **metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint**, where the first long bone of the foot meets the first bone of the big toe. The long bone shifts toward the inside of the foot, the big toe angles toward the second toe, and the joint gets larger and sticks out.<sup>[2]</sup> Because that joint bends with every step, the bigger the bunion gets, the more painful walking can become.<sup>[2]</sup>

In advanced cases, the big toe can go under or over the second toe, push the smaller toes out of line and lead to **hammer toes**, calluses, bursitis or arthritis in the joint.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Symptoms

- A **hard bump** on the side of the foot, by the big toe<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- A big toe that points toward the other toes<sup>[1]</sup>
- **Redness, swelling** or hard skin over the bump, and sometimes a corn or callus<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- Pain along the side or bottom of the foot<sup>[1]</sup>
- Stiffness and limited movement of the big toe, which can make walking difficult<sup>[2]</sup>

The NHS notes that red, hot, swollen skin over the joint that comes and goes is more typical of **gout**, and aching, stiff joints that are worse in the morning point more toward **arthritis**.<sup>[1]</sup> The doctor usually confirms the diagnosis with an **X-ray taken while you stand**, which shows how far out of line the bones are and whether there is arthritis.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What you can do to ease the pain

You can't get rid of a bunion or stop it getting worse on your own, but you can reduce the pain.<sup>[1]</sup> The NHS recommends:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Wearing **wide shoes with a low heel and a soft sole**, and avoiding high heels and tight, pointed shoes
- Holding a wrapped ice pack on the bunion for **up to 5 minutes** at a time
- Using **bunion pads** to stop shoes rubbing
- Taking acetaminophen or ibuprofen
- Losing weight if you are overweight

The AAOS adds that silicone pads should be tried for a short time first, because a pad that is too big can increase pressure on the bump. Toe spacers, shoe inserts and night splints can relieve pain, but splints have **not been shown to permanently fix bunions**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When surgery is considered

Surgery is an option when the bunion is **painful** and makes walking difficult despite changing shoes and other measures. It is **not done just to improve how the foot looks**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> The most common operation is an **osteotomy**: the surgeon removes the bump and realigns the bones, which are held with screws or small plates. It can be done as open surgery or with minimally invasive techniques, which may mean less early swelling, although long-term results are similar.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

Most people go home the same day, but recovery is long. The NHS says you will usually need to rest with your foot raised for **at least 2 weeks**, avoid driving for **6 to 8 weeks**, stay off work for **2 to 12 weeks** and avoid sports for **3 to 6 months**. Afterward, the toe may be weaker or stiffer, may not be perfectly straight, and bunions sometimes come back.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if the pain hasn't improved after a few weeks of home care, if it stops you doing your normal activities, if the bunions are getting worse, or if you have bunions and **diabetes**, because foot problems can be more serious with diabetes.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If a bunion is making every pair of shoes uncomfortable and home measures aren't enough, an assessment can help you control the pain and decide whether other treatment is worth considering.
