**Occipital neuralgia** is a type of headache caused by injury or inflammation of the **occipital nerves**, the nerves that run from the top of the neck up through the scalp. The ones involved are the **greater and lesser occipital nerves**, and compression of the **C2 and C3 nerve roots** in the neck can contribute to the pain.<sup>[1]</sup> It is not life-threatening, and the pain usually goes away once the nerve heals.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What it feels like

The pain is often described as **piercing, throbbing or like an electric shock** in the upper neck, the back of the head or behind the ears.<sup>[1]</sup> Typical features include:<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

- Continuous aching, burning or throbbing, with **brief shooting or shock-like pains**
- Pain that starts at the **base of the skull** and spreads over the scalp, on one or both sides
- Pain **behind the eye** on the affected side
- Pain triggered by a **light touch**, such as brushing your hair
- Episodes that last seconds or hours, or pain that becomes chronic

Because it can feel migraine-like, and some people have symptoms similar to migraine or cluster headaches, it is often hard to tell apart from other headache disorders.<sup>[1]</sup>

In some people no cause is found.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

There is **no single test** that confirms occipital neuralgia.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Diagnosis starts with a physical and neurological exam. If the results are not clear, the doctor may order an **MRI or CT scan** to rule out other causes. An **occipital nerve block**, an injection of numbing medicine near the nerve, can help confirm that the pain is coming from that nerve: if the pain goes away, the diagnosis is more likely.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## Treatment options

Treatment aims to relieve pain; the AANS notes it is not a cure.<sup>[1]</sup> It usually starts with the simplest measures.

<tr><td><strong>Home care</strong></td><td>Heat on the painful area, massage and stretching; chin tucks are suggested to help prevent flare-ups</td></tr>

<tr><td><strong>Botulinum toxin (Botox)</strong></td><td>Injections that may reduce nerve inflammation</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Nerve stimulation</strong></td><td>If other treatments fail: electrodes placed under the skin near the occipital nerves (occipital nerve stimulation) or along the spinal cord (spinal cord stimulation)</td></tr>

The AANS points out that occipital nerve stimulation is an off-label use of an FDA-approved device, and it cites success rates of roughly **40% to 50%** for high cervical spinal cord stimulation.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How long it takes to improve

According to Cleveland Clinic, **mild to moderate pain** often improves within **one to two weeks**, while more intense pain may take **four to six weeks**. Stretching or medicines may be needed afterward to keep the pain from coming back.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

See a doctor if the pain lasts or keeps coming back. Seek care **right away** if head or neck pain comes with:<sup>[2]</sup>

- Fever
- Double vision or loss of vision
- Weakness or numbness
- Trouble speaking
- Sudden confusion

> If a sharp, shock-like pain at the back of your head keeps returning, an assessment can confirm whether the occipital nerves are involved and whether a nerve block or another treatment could bring relief.
