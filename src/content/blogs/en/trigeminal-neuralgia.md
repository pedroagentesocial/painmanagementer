You brush your teeth, a breeze hits your cheek or you start to chew, and suddenly a stabbing pain, like an electric shock, shoots through one side of your face. A few seconds later it's gone, until the next one. This may be **trigeminal neuralgia (TN)**, a chronic pain disorder that involves **sudden attacks of severe facial pain**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## What is going on

TN affects the **trigeminal nerve**, the fifth cranial nerve, which carries sensation from the face and head. It has three branches (upper, middle and lower face); the **middle and lower branches** are the ones most often affected.<sup>[1]</sup>

According to NINDS, TN occurs **most often in people over age 50** and is **more common in women than in men**.<sup>[1]</sup> The NHS adds that it usually starts **between ages 50 and 60** and is rare in adults under 40.<sup>[2]</sup>

NINDS notes that the pain **rarely occurs during sleep**, and that in some people the condition is progressive, with longer and more frequent attacks.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Causes

- **Classic TN**, the most common form: a **blood vessel presses on the nerve root**.<sup>[1]</sup>
- **Secondary TN**: caused by another condition, such as **multiple sclerosis** or a tumor. NINDS says only **15% of cases** are secondary.<sup>[1]</sup>
- **Idiopathic TN**: no clear cause is found even after testing; it's the least common type.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

The workup usually includes a neurological exam and an **MRI** to look for nerve compression or other disorders that could cause facial pain.<sup>[1]</sup> The NHS recommends seeing a doctor about frequent or persistent facial pain, especially if usual painkillers don't help and a **dentist has ruled out dental causes**. It also warns that diagnosis can take a few years.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Treatment

**Medication is usually the first step.**<sup>[1]</sup> Ordinary pain relievers such as ibuprofen, and opioids, are usually **not helpful** for TN.<sup>[1]</sup>

Surgery is considered when medicines don't control the pain or cause side effects that are hard to tolerate.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>

> If you have sudden, shock-like attacks of facial pain and your dentist hasn't found a cause, an assessment can help you get the right diagnosis and a treatment plan that fits you.
