Pain after an operation is expected. According to a review published in *The Lancet* in 2019, acute pain is **almost universal** after surgery, but it **can be controlled and mostly resolves within a week**.<sup>[1]</sup> But for some people the pain **persists beyond the usual time of tissue healing** and becomes chronic.<sup>[1]</sup>

## What exactly is it?

The International Association for the Study of Pain (IASP), in the classification that is part of the WHO's ICD-11, defines **chronic postsurgical pain** as pain that **develops or increases in intensity after a surgical procedure** and **persists beyond the healing process — at least 3 months** after the operation. Other causes, such as infection or a tumor, must be ruled out first.<sup>[2]</sup>

## How common is it?

According to *The Lancet*:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Chronic postsurgical pain **bad enough to cause substantial functional impairment** occurs in **approximately 10%** of people who have surgery.
- It is considered **intolerable in about 1%**.
- An estimated **20% of children** have this kind of pain one year after surgery.
- **More than 320 million people** have surgery worldwide every year, which makes it a growing public health problem.

## Risk factors

The *Lancet* review groups risk factors into five domains: **demographic, genetic, clinical, surgery-related and psychological**, and explains that they are **interlinked**. For example, chronic pain before surgery is more common in women, and greater pain sensitivity often comes with anxiety or depression.<sup>[1]</sup>

Some factors **can be modified**, especially when surgery is elective: **body-mass index**, **pain before the operation** and some **other health conditions**. Others, such as demographics, genetics or pain sensitivity, cannot.<sup>[1]</sup> **Nerve injury during surgery** probably contributes in some cases.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Can it be prevented?

Optimizing pain management around surgery should help, but the evidence **is still inconclusive**: most medications studied to prevent it have been disappointing.<sup>[1]</sup> That's why it makes sense to:

1. **Talk to your surgeon before the operation** if you already have chronic pain, anxiety or depression.
2. **Treat existing pain** when surgery is planned.
3. **Follow up** on pain in the weeks after surgery, instead of waiting for it to "go away on its own."

## Treatment once the pain is chronic

According to *The Lancet*, the most successful treatments target the nerve component of pain: **gabapentin and pregabalin**, and **SNRI** antidepressants such as duloxetine and venlafaxine. Their effect is **variable**, and they don't work the same way for everyone.<sup>[1]</sup>

One important point: the review notes that people who have had surgery are at **increased risk of long-term opioid use**.<sup>[1]</sup> The CDC's 2022 guideline recommends favoring **nonopioid therapies** for subacute and chronic pain.<sup>[3]</sup>

> No medication should be taken without medical advice: each one has contraindications and side effects.

## When to see a doctor

If **3 months or more** have passed since your operation and the pain continues, gets worse, or keeps you from your normal life, it's not something to accept as "normal." It deserves an assessment.

> A specialized pain management approach can help identify the source of postsurgical pain and combine treatments to restore your quality of life.
