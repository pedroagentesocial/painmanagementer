Arthritis is one of the most common causes of joint pain. According to the CDC, from 2019 to 2021 **21.2% of U.S. adults — about 53.2 million people — had doctor-diagnosed arthritis**.<sup>[1]</sup>

When your knees, hips or hands hurt, the instinct is to move less. But the evidence says the opposite: **the right kind of physical activity is one of the most useful tools for reducing pain and staying mobile**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Who does it affect?

Arthritis rises clearly with age: almost half of adults 65 and older have been diagnosed with it.<sup>[1]</sup>

In addition, **88.3%** of adults with arthritis are 45 or older.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Why exercise helps

The CDC puts it this way: **joint-friendly** physical activity can improve pain, function, mood and quality of life for people with arthritis, and can **delay the onset of arthritis-related disability**.<sup>[2]</sup>

The CDC's 2022 clinical guideline on pain agrees: for **knee and hip osteoarthritis** it lists exercise (aerobic, aquatic or resistance) as a non-drug therapy, along with **weight loss** for the knee and **manual therapy** for the hip.<sup>[3]</sup>

### What kind of activity

The CDC recommends **low-impact** activities, which carry a low risk of injury because they don't twist or overstress the joints, such as:<sup>[2]</sup>

- Walking
- Biking
- Swimming

If you have arthritis or another health condition, the same report says you should be **as physically active as your health allows**.<sup>[1]</sup> You don't need to start at 150 minutes: what matters is starting and building up gradually.

## Weight matters too

For people with overweight or obesity, the CDC notes that **even a small amount of weight loss** can help lower arthritis-related pain and disability.<sup>[2]</sup>

## A worrying gap

Only about **half** of adults with arthritis say a provider has ever counseled them about physical activity, and **less than 5%** of those counseled are referred to a physical activity program.<sup>[2]</sup> In other words, many people are not getting guidance that could help them.

## Tips to get started

1. **Check first** if you have severe pain, swelling or a recent injury.
2. **Start gently** with short walks or water exercise.
3. **Add strength work** two days a week to protect your joints.
4. **Listen to your body:** mild discomfort is expected; sharp or worsening pain is not.
5. **Get support:** a guided physical therapy program helps you do it safely.

> A rehabilitation plan tailored to your joint and your pain level can help you move more, and move better.
