A broken wrist, a sprain or a surgery that should have healed in a few weeks. But the pain doesn't just linger: it's worse than at the start, your hand is swollen, it changes color and you can't stand even the touch of a bedsheet. This may be **complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS)**, which the NHS describes as a poorly understood condition in which a person has **persistent, severe and debilitating pain**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Why it happens

According to the NHS, **the cause is unknown**. Most cases are triggered by an **injury**, and it's thought the body reacts abnormally to it: the resulting pain is **much more severe and long-lasting than normal**.<sup>[1]</sup> It usually affects **one arm or one leg**, although it can sometimes spread to other parts of the body.<sup>[2]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

The NHS explains that diagnosis **can be difficult**, because it involves tests to **rule out other causes** of the pain. That's why some cases go undiagnosed or are mistaken for other problems.<sup>[1]</sup> If persistent pain is keeping you from your everyday activities, get it checked: **early treatment may help reduce symptoms**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Treatment

There is no cure yet, but there are treatments that help. Care usually involves **several professionals** and combines four types of support:<sup>[1]</sup>

<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Type</th><th>What it involves</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>Education and self-management</strong></td><td>Understanding the condition, staying active, managing pain flares, practicing relaxation and continuing rehab at home</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Physical rehabilitation</strong></td><td>Physical therapy to gradually increase activity without making pain worse; techniques such as <strong>graded motor imagery</strong>, <strong>mirror therapy</strong> and <strong>desensitization</strong> (touching the area with different textures)</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Medications</strong></td><td>Anti-inflammatories such as ibuprofen are usually tried first; anticonvulsants (gabapentin, pregabalin) and tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline, nortriptyline) are also used for nerve pain</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Psychological support</strong></td><td>Therapies such as cognitive behavioral therapy, often within pain management programs, to help you cope better with symptoms</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>

The NHS notes that opioids may be used for severe pain but are **often not very effective** for CRPS.<sup>[3]</sup> If medicines don't reduce the pain, **spinal cord stimulation** may be recommended: a device that sends mild electrical pulses to change how pain is felt.<sup>[3]</sup>

## Does it get better?

According to the NHS, **CRPS often gradually improves over time**, although some people have pain for many years.<sup>[1]</sup> Keeping the limb moving, even when it's hard, is a core part of recovery.<sup>[3]</sup>

> If after an injury you have pain that's out of proportion to what happened, with swelling or changes in skin color, an early assessment can help you start treatment as soon as possible.
