You get out of bed, put your foot down and feel a stab in your heel. After a short walk it eases, but it comes back after a while on your feet. That's the typical pattern of **plantar fasciitis**, which causes pain on the bottom of the foot, around the heel and arch. It comes from **straining the plantar fascia**, the tissue that connects the heel bone to the toes.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How to recognize it

According to the NHS, it is more likely to be plantar fasciitis if:<sup>[1]</sup>

- The pain is **much worse on your first steps** after sleeping or resting.
- It **eases once you start moving**, but returns after long periods of standing, walking or running.
- It **hurts to stretch the sole of your foot**, such as when raising your toes or climbing stairs.

## Risk factors

It isn't always clear why it happens. The NHS lists these factors:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Being **40 to 60 years old**
- Starting exercise on **hard surfaces**
- Having a **tight calf or heel**
- Overstretching the sole of the foot during exercise
- Doing **much more standing or walking than usual**
- Wearing **shoes with poor cushioning or support**
- Being **overweight**

## What the 2023 clinical guideline recommends

The clinical practice guideline published in 2023 in the *Journal of Orthopaedic & Sports Physical Therapy* reviews the evidence on physical therapy treatments for plantar fasciitis. These are its main recommendations:<sup>[2]</sup>

<tr><th>Treatment</th><th>What the guideline says</th><th class="num">Grade</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>Manual therapy</strong></td><td>Use it on the joints and soft tissues of the leg and foot to reduce pain and improve function</td><td class="num">A</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Stretching</strong></td><td>Of the plantar fascia and calf, to reduce pain and improve function in the short and long term</td><td class="num">A</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Foot taping</strong></td><td>Rigid or elastic, together with other treatments, for short-term improvement</td><td class="num">A</td></tr>

The practical takeaway: **orthoses can help, but they aren't enough on their own**. The guideline's strongest recommendations (grade A) are manual therapy, stretching, taping and night splints.<sup>[2]</sup>

If that doesn't help, the NHS mentions other options, such as **steroid injections** and **shock-wave therapy**. **Surgery is less common**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if:<sup>[1]</sup>

- The pain is **severe** or stops you from doing normal activities.
- The pain is **getting worse or keeps coming back**.
- It **hasn't improved after 2 weeks** of home care.
- You have **tingling or numbness** in your foot.
- You have **diabetes and foot pain**, as this can be more serious.

> If your heel hurts every morning, an assessment can confirm the cause and help you start the right physical therapy plan.
