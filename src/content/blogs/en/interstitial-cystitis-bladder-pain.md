It feels like a urinary tract infection that never ends: pain low in the belly, the urge to go **again and again**, and only a few drops each time. Yet the urine tests keep coming back normal. That can be **interstitial cystitis (IC)**, also called **bladder pain syndrome**, a long-lasting condition that causes pain in the bladder and the pelvis.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## How common it is

The NIH estimates that **between 4 and 12 million people** in the United States may have IC. The exact number isn't known because it can be mistaken for other conditions.<sup>[2]</sup> It is **more common in women** than in men,<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> and the NHS notes that it usually affects people **aged 40 or older**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Symptoms

Symptoms **come and go**: there are usually periods when they get worse, called **flare-ups**, and periods when they are better.<sup>[1]</sup> Some people feel only mild discomfort, while others have intense pain or struggle with urgency.<sup>[2]</sup>

People with IC are also more likely to have **other chronic pain conditions**, such as fibromyalgia, vulvodynia, migraine and chronic fatigue syndrome, as well as irritable bowel syndrome, allergies, and depression or anxiety.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Pain and nighttime trips to the bathroom can affect sleep, physical activity, social life and intimacy.<sup>[2]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

There is **no single test** for IC. The diagnosis is made by ruling out other conditions with similar symptoms, such as a urinary tract infection. Tests may include blood and urine tests, a pelvic exam, an ultrasound, a **cystoscopy** (a thin tube with a camera to look inside the bladder) and measuring the pressure inside the bladder.<sup>[1]</sup>

The NHS and the NIH recommend keeping a **food diary** to spot which foods and drinks trigger your flares.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[4]</sup> Staying well hydrated also helps; ask your doctor how much fluid is right for you.<sup>[3]</sup>

## Treatment

There is currently **no cure**, but treatments can help manage the symptoms. You may have to try several before you find what works best for you.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[3]</sup>

<div class="data-table">
<table>
<thead>
<tr><th>Option</th><th>What it involves</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td><strong>Lifestyle changes</strong></td><td>Avoiding trigger foods, staying hydrated, quitting smoking, gentle exercise such as walking or yoga, and relaxation techniques</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Bladder training</strong></td><td>Helps the bladder hold more urine, reduces pain and urgency, and means fewer trips to the bathroom</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Pelvic floor physical therapy</strong></td><td>A specialized physical therapist helps stretch and relax tight pelvic floor muscles</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Medicines</strong></td><td>Everyday pain relievers; nerve pain medicines such as amitriptyline, gabapentin or pregabalin; medicines that reduce the urge to urinate; and, if others fail, pentosan polysulfate sodium</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Bladder procedures</strong></td><td>Bladder instillation (liquid medicine placed in the bladder through a catheter), bladder stretching under anesthesia, or Botox injections into the bladder</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Nerve stimulation</strong></td><td>Devices that send mild electrical pulses to nerves, including sacral nerve stimulation, to reduce pain, frequency and urgency</td></tr>
<tr><td><strong>Surgery</strong></td><td>Rarely needed; enlarging or removing the bladder is considered only if severe pain persists after all other options</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>
<p class="source-note">Sources: NHS (2025); NIDDK, NIH (2024).<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[3]</sup></p>

Two important points from the NIH: if you have IC, avoid **pelvic floor strengthening exercises such as Kegels** unless you are working with a physical therapist, and nerve stimulators and Botox are **not FDA-approved for IC**, although a doctor may recommend them when other treatments haven't worked.<sup>[3]</sup> Surgery carries many risks and does not always cure the pain.<sup>[3]</sup> The NHS also lists help with managing long-term pain and mental health support for low mood or anxiety.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if you have pain in your bladder, lower belly or back that doesn't go away or keeps coming back, or if you are urinating much more than usual. Get **urgent** help if you see **blood in your urine**, or if you have that pain together with a **very high temperature** or chills, which could be signs of an infection.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If bladder pain is shaping your days and nights and the tests keep showing no infection, an assessment focused on pain can help you put together a plan that combines several treatments.
