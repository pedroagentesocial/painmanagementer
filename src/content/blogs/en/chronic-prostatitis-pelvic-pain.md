**Prostatitis** means inflammation of the prostate, a small gland that sits below the bladder.<sup>[1]</sup> Short-term (acute) prostatitis is usually caused by an infection and treated with antibiotics.<sup>[1]</sup> But when symptoms last **3 months or longer**, it is called chronic prostatitis.<sup>[1]</sup> The most common form, in which there is often no clear infection, is called **chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS)**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## How common it is

The AAFP reports that **between 2.2% and 9.7% of men** have symptoms of CP/CPPS, a prevalence it compares to that of heart disease or diabetes. CP/CPPS makes up about **90% of prostatitis cases**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What causes it

The cause of long-term prostatitis is often unknown. The NHS says it is thought to involve infection, inflammation or a combination of both.<sup>[1]</sup> The AAFP describes it as a condition in which urinary, muscle, digestive and psychological factors can all play a part, with pain as the main feature.<sup>[2]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

CP/CPPS is a **diagnosis of exclusion**: it is defined as genitourinary or pelvic pain that persists or comes back for **at least 3 of the past 6 months**, once other causes have been ruled out, such as urethritis, urinary tract disease, a narrowed urethra, nerve-related bladder problems or cancer.<sup>[2]</sup> Doctors often use a questionnaire, the **NIH Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index (NIH-CPSI)**, to measure symptoms and track changes over time.<sup>[2]</sup> Men with chronic symptoms may be referred to a urologist.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Treatment: a combination, not a single pill

The AAFP explains that treatment should be **individualized and multimodal**, aimed at improving the symptoms that bother each man most, and that few treatments are supported by strong evidence. Success is usually defined as a drop of **25% or 6 points** on the NIH-CPSI.<sup>[2]</sup> In the NIH classification the AAFP uses, antibiotics are listed for the bacterial types of prostatitis, while treatment of CP/CPPS is guided by the predominant symptoms.<sup>[2]</sup>

The NHS lists these options for chronic prostatitis:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Antibiotics and **other medicines**
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** or counseling
- **Physical therapy**
- Alternative therapies such as acupuncture, where available

To ease pain, the NHS suggests acetaminophen or ibuprofen and drinking plenty of fluids.<sup>[1]</sup>

## A few reassuring facts

- CP/CPPS **does not increase the risk of prostate cancer**.<sup>[2]</sup>
- It may be associated with male infertility, so it is worth mentioning to your doctor if you are trying to have children.<sup>[2]</sup>
- With acute bacterial prostatitis, most men feel better within **2 weeks** of starting antibiotics, although all symptoms can take several weeks to clear.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When to get help

See a doctor if you have any symptoms of prostatitis.<sup>[1]</sup> Get urgent help if you **can't pee at all**, or if your symptoms haven't improved or have gotten worse **48 hours** after starting antibiotics.<sup>[1]</sup>

Call 911 if you have sudden confusion, slurred speech, blue, gray, pale or blotchy skin or lips, a rash that doesn't fade when you press a glass on it, or severe difficulty breathing, because these can be signs of a serious infection or sepsis.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If pelvic pain has dragged on for months and rounds of antibiotics haven't fixed it, an assessment can look at the whole picture, from muscles to nerves to stress, and build a plan around the symptoms that bother you most.
