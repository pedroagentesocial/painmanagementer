When something hurts, the first thing many of us do is open the medicine cabinet. **Acetaminophen** (called **paracetamol** outside the U.S.) and **ibuprofen** are among the most widely used pain medicines. The FDA reminds us that **they are safe and effective when label directions are followed**, but that **taking more than recommended can cause serious injury**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Acetaminophen: watch for the hidden dose

According to the FDA, acetaminophen is found in **hundreds of medicines**, both over-the-counter and prescription, sometimes as the only active ingredient and sometimes combined with others. On prescription labels it may be abbreviated as **APAP** or **Acetaminoph**.<sup>[1]</sup> That's why it's easy to take too much without realizing it.

The FDA recommends:<sup>[1]</sup>

1. **Reading the label** of every medicine before you take it.
2. **Not using more than one acetaminophen-containing product at a time.**
3. Following the dose, the hours between doses and the **daily limit** on the label. For adults and children 12 and older, the total in 24 hours **should not exceed 4,000 mg**, and never more than your label says.
4. **Not taking more** even if you still have pain or fever.
5. Asking first if you have **liver disease**.
6. Being aware that severe liver damage may occur if you have **3 or more alcoholic drinks a day** while using acetaminophen.

If you think you've taken too much, get medical help or call **Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222** right away.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Ibuprofen: who should check first

According to the NHS, ibuprofen **may not be suitable** if you:<sup>[3]</sup>

- Have or have had a **stomach ulcer**.
- Have a **heart, liver or kidney** condition.
- Have **asthma** or allergies.
- Have **blood-clotting** problems or have had a **stroke**.
- Are **pregnant** or trying to get pregnant.

The NHS also advises taking it **with or after food**, **not combining it with other NSAIDs** such as naproxen or aspirin, and **not taking it for more than 10 days** unless a doctor says so.<sup>[3]</sup> Adults can take it together with acetaminophen if needed.<sup>[3]</sup> It can also interact with some blood pressure medicines.<sup>[3]</sup>

## Warning signs

Get urgent care if, while taking ibuprofen, you notice signs of **stomach bleeding**: stomach pain, shortness of breath, pale skin, **vomiting blood** or **black, sticky stools**.<sup>[3]</sup>

## When the medicine cabinet isn't enough

These medicines are meant for **short-term pain**. If you need them almost every day, or the pain comes back as soon as they wear off, the NHS recommends getting checked.<sup>[3]</sup> Pain that lasts for weeks deserves a look at its cause and a plan that doesn't rely on pills alone.

> If you've been taking pain relievers for the same pain for weeks, an assessment can help find the cause and a safer long-term treatment.
