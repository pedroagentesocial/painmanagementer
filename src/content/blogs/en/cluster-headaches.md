A **cluster headache** is not an ordinary headache. It is a sharp, burning or piercing pain, usually on **one side of the head, around the eye**, that starts and stops quickly and without warning.<sup>[1]</sup> The attacks come in groups, or "clusters": they can happen several times a day for weeks or months and then disappear for a while.<sup>[1]</sup> They are not caused by another illness, and although there is no cure, treatments can help.<sup>[1]</sup>

## What an attack looks like

Each attack lasts about **15 minutes to 3 hours**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> MedlinePlus explains that the pain peaks within **5 to 10 minutes**, and the most intense part usually lasts **30 minutes to 2 hours**.<sup>[2]</sup> Attacks often come at the **same time of day**, and they frequently start **2 to 3 hours after falling asleep**, although they can also happen while awake.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

During an attack, people tend to feel **restless and need to walk around or move**.<sup>[1]</sup> On the same side as the pain there may also be:<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

- A red, watering eye
- A drooping or swollen eyelid
- A smaller pupil
- A sweating or flushed face
- A blocked or runny nose

## Bouts and pain-free periods

Attacks occur daily or almost daily during a bout, and the pain-free periods between bouts last **at least a month**.<sup>[2]</sup> The NHS notes that the breaks can last weeks, months or even years, while some people have no gap at all between episodes.<sup>[1]</sup> Bouts often return at the same time of year.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Who gets them

Cluster headaches are **rare**. The NHS says they are more common in **men in their 30s and 40s** and in people with a close family member who has them.<sup>[1]</sup> MedlinePlus adds that they can begin at any age, and that more men than women are affected.<sup>[2]</sup>

The cause is not known.<sup>[1]</sup> There is no specific test: the diagnosis is based on how the pain behaves, and a doctor may order a brain scan or MRI to rule out other causes.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

Both sources recommend keeping a **headache diary** to help identify your own triggers.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## Why ordinary painkillers don't work

The NHS is clear that painkillers such as **acetaminophen (paracetamol) and ibuprofen do not work** for cluster headaches.<sup>[1]</sup> MedlinePlus explains that pain medicines, including narcotics, usually act **too slowly** to help an attack that peaks within minutes.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Preventing attacks

During a bout, a doctor may prescribe medicines to prevent attacks, such as **verapamil, corticosteroids or lithium**.<sup>[1]</sup> MedlinePlus also lists steroid injections, certain blood pressure, seizure and antidepressant medicines, and **CGRP-blocking medicines**.<sup>[2]</sup> Avoiding known triggers is part of prevention too.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> When nothing else has worked, an implanted stimulation device is sometimes considered.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

Cluster headaches are not life-threatening and usually don't cause lasting brain changes, but they can be painful enough to disrupt work and daily life. They may become less frequent with age.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When to get help

See a doctor if you think you have cluster headaches, if headaches keep coming back, or if painkillers don't help.<sup>[1]</sup> Get help urgently if a severe headache comes with **jaw pain when eating, blurred or double vision, a sore scalp, or numbness or weakness** in the arms or legs, because these can be signs of temporal arteritis.<sup>[1]</sup>

Call 911 if you have a drooping eyelid or a smaller pupil in one eye **for the first time**, even if you have been diagnosed before,<sup>[1]</sup> or if you have the worst headache of your life, a sudden headache with problems speaking, seeing or moving, repeated vomiting, or a high fever with a stiff neck.<sup>[2]</sup>

> If attacks keep waking you up at night and over-the-counter pills do nothing, an assessment can confirm the diagnosis and get you a fast-acting plan for the next bout.
