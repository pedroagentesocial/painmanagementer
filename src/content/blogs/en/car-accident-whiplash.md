One of the most common injuries after a crash, especially a rear-end collision, is **whiplash**.

## What is whiplash?

The U.S. National Library of Medicine (MedlinePlus) defines it as **a soft tissue injury to the neck**, also called a neck sprain or strain. It happens when the head is suddenly jerked forward and back. MedlinePlus notes that falls and accidents, **including car accidents**, are a common cause of neck pain.<sup>[2]</sup>

### Symptoms to watch for

According to the NHS, the most common symptoms are:<sup>[5]</sup>

- Neck pain
- Neck stiffness and difficulty moving your head
- Headaches
- Pain and muscle spasms in the shoulders and arms

Symptoms **can take several hours to start** after the injury.<sup>[5]</sup> The NHS advises getting urgent help for **severe pain that painkillers don't ease, tingling or pins and needles, trouble walking or sitting upright, a sudden "electric shock" feeling in the neck and back, or weakness in the hands, arms or legs**.<sup>[5]</sup>

Neck pain can also come from the shoulder, jaw, head or upper arms, which is why an assessment that pinpoints the source matters.<sup>[2]</sup> And if the impact also affected your back, NIAMS recommends seeing a doctor for **back pain after a fall or injury**, numbness, tingling or leg weakness.<sup>[4]</sup>

## How long does recovery take?

The NHS says whiplash **usually gets better within 2 to 3 months**, although some people have symptoms for longer.<sup>[5]</sup> The most recent systematic review by the OPTIMa collaboration (Ontario Protocol for Traffic Injury Management), published in 2020, found that:<sup>[3]</sup>

- **Half** of people with whiplash-associated disorders **improve substantially within 3 months**.
- **Half** are **symptom-free within 6 months**.

That also means a significant share of patients still have symptoms beyond that point. The same review found that **post-crash psychological factors**, such as recovery expectations and coping, are associated with how recovery goes.<sup>[3]</sup>

## How is it treated?

According to MedlinePlus, treatment for neck injuries depends on the cause and may include **ice, pain relievers or physical therapy**; surgery is rarely needed.<sup>[2]</sup>

For whiplash specifically, the NHS advises **keeping up everyday activities even if they hurt a little**, because that speeds recovery, and **avoiding long periods of resting the neck and using a neck collar**, which does not help.<sup>[5]</sup> Ideally, a professional should tell you what to do in your case.

In practice, a good plan usually combines:

1. **A medical assessment** to rule out injuries that need a different kind of care.
2. **Guided mobility work and physical therapy** to restore range of motion and strength.
3. **Pain control** tailored to each case.
4. **Emotional support** when the accident leaves fear, anxiety or sleep problems, since these factors affect recovery.<sup>[3]</sup>

## Why not wait

- Symptoms can take **hours** to appear, so feeling fine right after the crash doesn't rule out an injury.<sup>[5]</sup>
- An **early medical assessment** documents the injuries and how they progress.
- If pain lasts more than a few weeks, get it checked: you don't have to get used to living with it.

> If you were in a car accident and have neck, back or head pain, an assessment can help you understand the injury and start recovery with a clear plan.
