You've started running more, or you've changed shoes, and now the back of your ankle hurts and feels stiff when you get out of bed. The day after exercising, the pain is worse. This may be **Achilles tendinitis**, a common condition in which the large tendon at the back of the lower leg becomes **irritated and inflamed**.<sup>[1]</sup>

The AAOS explains that "tendinitis" refers to inflammation, while "tendinopathy" refers to chronic, microscopic wear of the tendon; the two terms are often used interchangeably.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Why it happens

It's usually not caused by a specific injury but by **repetitive stress** on the tendon, often from **doing too much, too soon**. The AAOS lists these factors:<sup>[1]</sup>

- A **sudden increase** in the distance, duration or intensity of exercise
- A **change in footwear**
- **Tight calf muscles**, which put more stress on the tendon
- **Haglund's deformity**, an enlarged bone on the back of the heel that rubs against the tendon

## Symptoms

- Pain and stiffness along the tendon **in the morning**<sup>[1]</sup>
- Pain that **worsens with activity**, or severe pain the day after exercising<sup>[1]</sup>
- Thickening of the tendon, and swelling that gets worse during the day<sup>[1]</sup>
- Pain at the back of the heel when wearing shoes<sup>[1]</sup>

If you feel a **sudden "pop"** in the calf or heel, the tendon may have torn: **see a doctor right away**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

The doctor checks for swelling, the exact spot of the pain and how well you can point your toes. **X-rays** can show bone spurs; an **MRI** isn't needed for the diagnosis, but it helps plan surgery if other treatments fail. **Ultrasound** is another option.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Treatment without surgery

The AAOS notes that most people get acceptable pain relief without surgery, although improvement **can take months**, and pain may last **more than 3 months** even with early treatment.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Why cortisone injections are not recommended

The AAOS states that **cortisone injections are not recommended** in the Achilles tendon, because they can **cause it to rupture** and reduce its strength.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When surgery is considered

Surgery is only considered if the pain **doesn't improve after 6 months** of nonsurgical treatment. Depending on the case, damaged tissue and bone spurs may be removed, or the calf muscle may be lengthened. According to the AAOS, up to **75%** of patients who have the damaged tissue removed return to their previous activity level, but rehabilitation can take **up to 12 months**.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If the back of your heel has hurt for weeks and you can't get back to your usual activity, an assessment can help you build a recovery plan without making the tendon worse.
