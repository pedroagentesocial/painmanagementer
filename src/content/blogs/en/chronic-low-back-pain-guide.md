If your lower back hurts, you are far from alone. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that **619 million people** had low back pain worldwide in 2020, and that the number will reach about **843 million by 2050**, mainly because the population is growing and aging.<sup>[1]</sup> It is also **the leading cause of disability worldwide**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When is it acute, and when is it chronic?

The WHO classifies low back pain by how long it lasts:<sup>[1]</sup>

<tr><th>Type</th><th>Duration</th></tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>Acute</td><td>Less than 6 weeks</td></tr>
<tr><td>Subacute</td><td>6 to 12 weeks</td></tr>
<tr><td>Chronic</td><td>More than 12 weeks</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>
</div>
<p class="source-note">Source: WHO (2023).<sup>[1]</sup></p>

It can start at any age, even in children and teenagers. The highest number of cases occurs between **ages 50 and 55**, and women are affected more often than men.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Most common causes

According to the U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIAMS), back pain can come from several sources, which sometimes overlap:<sup>[4]</sup>

- **Mechanical or structural:** sprains and strains, degenerative disc disease, herniated discs, spondylolisthesis, spinal stenosis, vertebral fractures, scoliosis or myofascial pain.
- **Inflammatory:** such as ankylosing spondylitis and other inflammatory arthritis of the spine.
- **Other conditions:** osteoporosis, fibromyalgia, kidney stones or infections, endometriosis, spinal infections, (rare) tumors, or pregnancy.

In many cases no specific cause is found; this is called **non-specific low back pain**.<sup>[1]</sup>

### Risk factors

NIAMS lists, among others: low fitness or weak core muscles, overexertion after a period of inactivity, weight gain, physically demanding jobs, desk work with poor posture, chronic poor sleep, depression or anxiety, age (especially after 45), and heredity.<sup>[4]</sup>

## Warning signs: when to see a doctor

NIAMS recommends seeing a doctor if:<sup>[4]</sup>

- The pain **doesn't improve after a few weeks**.
- You have **numbness or tingling**.
- The pain is **severe and doesn't improve with medication**.
- It started **after a fall or an injury**.
- It comes with **trouble urinating, weakness or numbness in the legs, fever, or unexplained weight loss**.

Those last symptoms call for prompt medical care.

In December 2023 the WHO published its **first guideline** on the non-surgical management of chronic primary low back pain in adults.<sup>[2]</sup> As summarized by UN News, the guideline:<sup>[3]</sup>

The WHO also stresses that care should be tailored to each person's mix of factors.<sup>[3]</sup> Its fact sheet adds that painkillers **should not be the first-line treatment** and, when used, are best combined with other measures: physical therapy, psychological and social support, reducing strain during physical work, and habits such as more physical activity, a healthy diet and good sleep.<sup>[1]</sup>

## The bottom line

- Low back pain is very common, but **once it lasts more than 12 weeks it is chronic** and deserves a plan, not just painkillers.
- **Exercise, education and psychological support** are the foundation of what the WHO recommends.
- If you notice **warning signs**, don't wait: see a professional.

> An assessment looks for the source of your pain so you and your care team can decide which combination of treatments makes sense for you.
