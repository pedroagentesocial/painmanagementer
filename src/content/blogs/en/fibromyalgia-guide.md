Fibromyalgia is a condition you can barely see from the outside but feel deeply on the inside. The U.S. National Institutes of Health (NIH) describe it as a disorder with **chronic, widespread pain throughout the body or in multiple areas**, together with **fatigue and sleep problems**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How many people have it?

An analysis of the 2012 U.S. National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), published by NIH researchers, estimated that **1.75% of adults — about 3.94 million people —** had symptoms consistent with fibromyalgia criteria. A striking finding: **73% of them reported a physician's diagnosis other than fibromyalgia**.<sup>[3]</sup>

In other words, many people live with these symptoms without knowing what is going on.

## Who gets it

According to NIAMS:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Anyone can get it, but **it is more common in women** than in men.
- It usually starts in **middle age**, and the chance of having it rises with age; children can get it too.
- It occurs in all racial and ethnic groups.

## Symptoms

Besides widespread pain, fatigue and sleep problems, NIAMS lists:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Muscle and joint stiffness
- Tenderness to touch
- Numbness or tingling
- Problems with memory and concentration (so-called **"fibro fog"**)
- Increased sensitivity to light, noise, odors and temperature
- Digestive problems

## What causes it?

The exact cause **is unknown**. Studies show that people with fibromyalgia have an **increased sensitivity to pain**, and brain imaging points to changes in how pain signals are processed.<sup>[1]</sup>

Risk factors NIAMS mentions include having other conditions such as **rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, ankylosing spondylitis, osteoarthritis, depression or anxiety, chronic back pain or irritable bowel syndrome**, as well as family history.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How is it diagnosed?

**There is no specific lab or imaging test** for fibromyalgia. Diagnosis is based mainly on having **pain all over the body along with other symptoms**. The doctor reviews your medical history, examines you and may order tests to **rule out other conditions**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Treatments that help

There is no cure, but symptoms **can be managed**. Treatment usually **combines several strategies**:<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

The CDC's 2022 clinical guideline agrees: for fibromyalgia it lists **cognitive behavioral therapy, myofascial release massage, mindfulness, tai chi, qigong, acupuncture and multidisciplinary rehabilitation**, and warns that **the benefits of opioids are unlikely to outweigh their risks** for this condition.<sup>[4]</sup>

## The bottom line

- Fibromyalgia **is real**: there is increased sensitivity to pain; it is not "exaggerating."<sup>[1]</sup>
- Many people with matching symptoms **carry a different diagnosis** or don't know they have it.<sup>[3]</sup>
- What works best is **combining** gradual exercise, psychological support, good sleep and, when needed, medication.

> If you've had pain in several parts of your body for months, along with fatigue and poor sleep, an assessment can help you put a name to it and start a plan that works for you.
