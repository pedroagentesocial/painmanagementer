You walk a few blocks and the burning or heaviness in your legs starts. You sit down or lean forward, for example on a shopping cart, and it eases. That's a typical pattern of **spinal stenosis**, which according to the NIH happens when **the spaces inside the spine narrow and put pressure on the spinal cord and nerve roots**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Who gets it

Anyone can get it, but **the chances increase with age**. Younger people can have it too if they were born with a narrow spinal canal or had a spine injury. It is most common in the **lower back** and the **neck**.<sup>[1]</sup>

**Sitting or flexing the lower back or neck may relieve symptoms.** Leaning forward usually eases leg pain, while standing or arching the back makes it worse.<sup>[1]</sup> In more severe cases there may be **bowel, bladder or sexual function problems**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Causes

According to NIAMS:<sup>[1]</sup>

- **Aging:** thickened ligaments, bulging disks, bone spurs and spondylolisthesis (a vertebra slipping forward).
- **Arthritis:** mainly osteoarthritis and, less commonly, rheumatoid arthritis.
- **Inherited conditions:** being born with a narrow canal, scoliosis or achondroplasia.
- **Others:** fractures from injuries, spinal tumors or Paget's disease of bone.

## How it is diagnosed

Your doctor asks about your symptom history and checks range of motion, whether symptoms change when you bend, nerve function, balance and walking. They may order an **X-ray, MRI or CT scan**. NIAMS notes that **imaging alone does not determine whether treatment is needed**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When is surgery considered?

If symptoms continue despite treatment, your doctor may recommend seeing a surgeon. Surgery may be recommended **sooner** if there is **numbness or weakness that interferes with walking, impaired bladder or bowel function, or other neurological problems**.<sup>[2]</sup> According to NIAMS, most people who have surgery **have less leg pain and walk better**, although if the nerves were badly damaged some pain or numbness may remain.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Living with spinal stenosis

NIAMS recommends:<sup>[2]</sup>

1. **Exercising at least 3 times a week for 30 minutes**, adapting activities so they don't make pain worse (check with your doctor first).
2. **Pacing activities** and adjusting routines that trigger pain.
3. Using **walking aids** if they help your stability.
4. Trying **posture changes**: flexing the spine relieves some people.
5. Keeping a **healthy weight** and **quitting smoking**.

> If leg pain makes you stop every time you walk, an assessment can confirm whether it's spinal stenosis and help keep you moving.
