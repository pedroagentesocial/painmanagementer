Most back pain gets better with rest. But if you wake up with a stiff lower back, it takes you a while to get going in the morning and **the pain eases once you move**, there may be something else going on. That pattern is typical of **ankylosing spondylitis**, which according to the NIH is **a type of arthritis that causes inflammation in the joints and ligaments of the spine**.<sup>[1]</sup>

It can also cause **eye inflammation (uveitis)**, with eye pain and vision changes, as well as fatigue, loss of appetite or weight, psoriasis rashes, and abdominal pain or loose stools.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Who is at higher risk

The cause isn't fully known; **genes and the environment** are both thought to play a part. According to NIAMS, the risk is higher if you:<sup>[1]</sup>

- Carry the **HLA-B27** gene, although many people with this gene never develop the disease.
- Have **relatives** with ankylosing spondylitis.
- Have **Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis or psoriasis**.

## How it is diagnosed

Your doctor asks about your history and examines your spine, pelvis, heels and chest, as well as your flexibility and how your ribs move when you breathe deeply.<sup>[2]</sup> NIAMS points out that:<sup>[2]</sup>

- **No single test** confirms the diagnosis.
- **X-rays** may not show changes early on, because they can take years to appear.
- An **MRI** can help detect it in the early stages.
- **Blood tests** may check for the HLA-B27 gene and markers of inflammation.

There's no cure, but it can be managed. The options NIAMS describes are:<sup>[2]</sup>

## What you can do

NIAMS recommends:<sup>[2]</sup>

1. **Exercising regularly**, ideally low-impact, such as water exercise (check with your doctor first).
2. Paying attention to **posture** and using aids if bending is hard.
3. Watching your symptoms for **changes or flares**.
4. **Managing stress** with relaxation, yoga or tai chi, and asking for support if you feel anxious or down.
5. Keeping a **healthy weight** and **quitting smoking**: the disease is more severe in people who smoke, and smoking blunts the effect of treatment.

According to NIAMS, people who **take part in their own care** report less pain and make fewer doctor visits, and with treatment **most people lead productive lives**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

> If you're under 45 and have back pain that wakes you up stiff and gets better when you move, an assessment can help find out whether it's inflammatory and treat it in time.
