It feels like there is **a pebble or a marble stuck under your foot**, and the pain burns or stabs between your toes when you walk. This is typical of **Morton's neuroma**, a thickening of the tissue around one of the small nerves that run to the toes.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Despite its name, it is **not a true tumor**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Where it happens and why

It most often develops between the **third and fourth toes**, and less often between the second and third.<sup>[2]</sup> The exact cause is not known; research points to **compression of the nerve** as it passes under a ligament between the toe bones, which can cause swelling and abnormal tissue around it.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Symptoms

- **Shooting, stabbing or burning pain** in the ball of the foot or between the toes, especially when walking or running<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- The feeling of **a pebble or lump** under the foot<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- **Tingling or numbness** in the toes<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- Pain that is worse with tight shoes or high heels<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

The NHS notes that symptoms often get worse over time.<sup>[1]</sup> There is usually no visible lump, and pain at night is rare.<sup>[2]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

The doctor examines the foot and presses on the spaces between the toes. A local anesthetic injection can also help confirm the diagnosis if it relieves the pain right away. X-rays help rule out a stress fracture or arthritis, and **ultrasound** is the preferred imaging test; an MRI is usually not needed.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What you can do at home

The NHS recommends resting and raising the foot, taking ibuprofen or acetaminophen for pain, wearing **wide, comfortable shoes with a low heel and a soft sole**, using soft insoles or pads, losing weight if you are overweight, and cutting back on high-impact activities such as running and jumping.<sup>[1]</sup>

There are two main types of surgery: **decompression**, which frees the nerve and may preserve sensation, and **neurectomy**, which removes part of the nerve and can cause permanent numbness in the area. Symptoms come back and need more treatment in about **5% to 20%** of patients. After surgery, high-impact activities are usually avoided until the wound has healed, at least **2 to 3 weeks**, and many people can wear normal shoes by **4 weeks**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if the pain is severe or stops you doing your normal activities, is getting worse or keeps coming back, hasn't improved after **2 weeks** of self-care, if you have tingling, weakness or numbness in the foot, or if the pain started after an injury or surgery on the foot or ankle.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If every step feels like walking on a pebble and changing shoes hasn't been enough, an assessment can confirm the diagnosis and help you choose the next step in treatment.
