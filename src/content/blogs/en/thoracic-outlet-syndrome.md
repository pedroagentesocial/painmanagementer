**Thoracic outlet syndrome (TOS)** is a group of rare disorders that happen when nerves or blood vessels are **compressed in the lower neck and upper chest**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> The **thoracic outlet** is the narrow space between the collarbone and the first rib. Through it pass the **brachial plexus** (the bundle of nerves that runs to the arm) and the **subclavian artery and vein**.<sup>[1]</sup> When an injury or a difference in anatomy narrows that space, it can cause pain, tingling and other symptoms in the neck, shoulder, arm and hand.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Symptoms to watch for

Symptoms usually affect **one side** of the upper body.<sup>[1]</sup> They can include:<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

- Pain in the neck and shoulder
- Numbness and tingling in the **pinky and ring fingers** and the inner forearm
- A weak grip or weakness in the hand muscles
- Signs of poor circulation: a cold, swollen, pale or bluish hand
- Sores on the fingers that are slow to heal

A useful clue: TOS pain tends to get **worse when you raise the affected arm**, for example when you reach overhead or do your hair.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Causes and risk factors

Cleveland Clinic groups the causes into three kinds:<sup>[1]</sup>

- **Congenital:** differences you are born with, such as an extra rib in the neck (a cervical rib) or unusual muscles or ligaments
- **Traumatic:** a sudden injury, such as **whiplash from a car accident**
- **Functional:** repeated movements, such as vigorous arm motions in sports or lifting overhead again and again

Many cases appear **between ages 20 and 50**. Sports with repeated arm and shoulder movement (baseball, swimming, golf, volleyball), weightlifting and neck or back injuries raise the risk.<sup>[1]</sup> MedlinePlus adds that a long neck and drooping shoulders may also play a part.<sup>[2]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

There is no single test. The provider asks about your history and symptoms and does a physical exam, which may include moving your arms, turning or tilting your head and opening and closing your hands with your arms raised to see if the symptoms appear.<sup>[1]</sup> Depending on the type suspected, tests may include:<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

- X-rays, ultrasound, CT or MRI
- Imaging of the blood vessels (CT angiogram or MR angiogram)
- Electromyography (EMG) and nerve conduction studies

Part of the work is **ruling out other conditions** with similar symptoms, such as carpal tunnel syndrome.<sup>[2]</sup>

<tr><td><strong>Clot medicines</strong></td><td>Medicines that dissolve clots or blood thinners, for the venous and arterial types</td></tr>

Surgery relieves symptoms for many people, but in others they can come back.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What you can do day to day

Cleveland Clinic recommends avoiding **heavy bags on your shoulder** and lifting overhead, keeping up the exercises from your physical therapist at home to strengthen your muscles and improve your posture, and changing the activities that bring on the symptoms.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When to get help

Don't wait for the symptoms to go away on their own: early diagnosis and treatment usually lead to better results, and untreated TOS can cause clots, chronic arm swelling and permanent nerve damage.<sup>[1]</sup> Get **prompt care** if your hand or fingers turn **cold, pale or blue**, if your arm swells suddenly, or if you have finger sores that don't heal.<sup>[1]</sup> Call **911** if arm weakness comes with **slurred speech or a drooping face**, which can be signs of a stroke.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If you have neck and shoulder pain with tingling in your hand that gets worse when you lift your arm, especially after a car accident or with sports, an assessment can find out whether a nerve is being compressed and which treatment fits.
