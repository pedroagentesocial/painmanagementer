When headaches are frequent, it makes sense to reach for a painkiller. But taking acute headache medicine too often can backfire: it can lead to **medication overuse headache**, sometimes called rebound headache. NHS Scotland defines it as headache on **15 or more days a month** in someone who already had a headache disorder, such as migraine, and who has been regularly overusing acute medicines for **3 months**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How common it is

A meta-analysis published in 2025, which pooled data from **41,614 adults aged 18 to 65 in 17 countries**, estimated that about **4.1%** of adults have probable medication overuse headache. That is higher than the 1% to 2% suggested by earlier estimates.<sup>[2]</sup> It was more common in women (**5.6%**) than in men (**2.3%**).<sup>[2]</sup>

## How many days are too many

The limit depends on the type of medicine. These are the thresholds that, kept up over time, are linked to medication overuse headache:<sup>[1]</sup>

<tr><td><strong>Triptans</strong></td><td>Migraine medicines such as sumatriptan</td><td>10 or more days a month</td></tr>

To prevent it, the guidance advises keeping simple painkillers and triptans to **fewer than 10 days a month**, and **avoiding combination painkillers and opioids** for headache.<sup>[1]</sup> It also notes that rimegepant, a newer migraine medicine, is not expected to cause medication overuse headache.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How it is treated

The key step is **withdrawing the overused medicine**, ideally with a doctor's plan. NHS Scotland recommends:<sup>[1]</sup>

- **Stopping abruptly** for simple painkillers and triptans
- **Reducing gradually** for combination painkillers, especially those with high doses of codeine, and for opioids
- If stopping at once is not possible, cutting acute medicine down to **2 days a week**
- Staying well hydrated; anti-sickness medicine may be considered during withdrawal
- Starting a **preventive treatment** at the same time as the withdrawal

Most oral preventive medicines work less well while medicines are being overused. Options that are less affected include gepants, botulinum toxin A, CGRP monoclonal antibodies and topiramate.<sup>[1]</sup>

## What to expect during withdrawal

Headaches often **get worse before they get better**, and this can last days or weeks. Withdrawal can also bring nausea, trouble sleeping and anxiety.<sup>[1]</sup>

## When to talk to a doctor

Talk to a doctor if you have headaches on most days, or if you find yourself taking painkillers for headache on **10 or more days a month**. Don't stop opioids or high-dose codeine products suddenly on your own: these need to be reduced gradually.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If you are taking painkillers more and more often and the headaches aren't getting any better, a review of your medicines and a prevention plan can help break the cycle.
