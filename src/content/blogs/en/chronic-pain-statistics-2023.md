Chronic pain is neither rare nor isolated. According to the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), run by the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics, **24.3% of U.S. adults had chronic pain in 2023** — nearly 1 in 4 people.<sup>[1]</sup>

This article summarizes what that official data shows, how it is defined, and why it matters when you are looking for treatment.

## What counts as "chronic pain"?

In the CDC survey, a person has **chronic pain** when they felt pain on most days or every day in the past 3 months. It is **high-impact chronic pain** when that pain also frequently limited their life or work activities over the same period.<sup>[1]</sup>

The distinction matters: not all chronic pain stops you from working or doing chores, but high-impact pain does, and it is the kind that hurts quality of life the most.

Two more findings from the same report:

- Among people with chronic pain, **34.9% have high-impact pain** — more than a third are frequently limited in daily life.<sup>[1]</sup>
- **Women** report more chronic pain (25.4%) than **men** (23.2%), and more high-impact pain too (9.6% vs. 7.3%).<sup>[1]</sup>

## Pain increases with age

Age is one of the strongest factors. In 2023, chronic pain affected 12.3% of adults aged 18 to 29, but **36.0% of adults 65 and older**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Living outside big cities matters too

## Is the problem growing?

Within the same survey, chronic pain went from 20.4% of adults in 2019 to 24.3% in 2023.<sup>[1]</sup> An earlier CDC analysis of 2019–2021 data had already estimated that in 2021 about **51.6 million adults** lived with chronic pain and **17.1 million** with high-impact chronic pain.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What this data means for you

1. **Living with daily pain is not "normal."** Being common doesn't mean you have to accept it: if pain lasts more than 3 months or limits your activities, it deserves an assessment.
2. **There are options beyond painkillers.** The CDC's 2022 clinical guideline states that nonopioid therapies are preferred for subacute and chronic pain, and asks clinicians to make the most of non-drug treatments.<sup>[3]</sup>
3. **The goal is function, not just pain.** Guidelines focus on improving pain *and* your ability to live your life: work, sleep, move.<sup>[3]</sup>

> If your pain has lasted for weeks and keeps you from doing what you used to do, a medical assessment can help identify the cause and choose the right treatment.
