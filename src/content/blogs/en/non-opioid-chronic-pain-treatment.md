For years, opioids were often prescribed for pain that wouldn't go away. Today clinical guidelines point in another direction. The **CDC's 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline** — the official U.S. reference for prescribing opioids — states that **"nonopioid therapies are preferred for subacute and chronic pain."**<sup>[1]</sup>

That doesn't mean pain goes untreated. It means there is a wide range of options, with and without medication, that should be used first.

## The two key recommendations

The CDC guideline contains 12 recommendations. The first two set the overall approach:<sup>[1]</sup>

1. **Acute pain:** nonopioid therapies are at least as effective as opioids for many common types of acute pain. Clinicians should make the most of non-drug treatments and nonopioid medications, and only consider opioids if benefits are expected to outweigh risks.
2. **Subacute (1 to 3 months) and chronic (more than 3 months) pain:** nonopioid therapies are preferred. Opioids should only be considered if expected benefits for pain *and function* outweigh the risks.

In both cases, the guideline asks clinicians to discuss realistic benefits and known risks with patients before starting treatment.<sup>[1]</sup>

On fibromyalgia, the guideline is especially clear: the benefits of opioids are unlikely to outweigh their risks.<sup>[1]</sup>

## The WHO agrees

The WHO's first guideline on chronic low back pain, published in December 2023, also recommends exercise, education, psychological support, spinal manipulation, massage and NSAIDs, and **advises against opioid painkillers**, traction and lumbar braces.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Why this approach matters

With nearly 1 in 4 U.S. adults living with chronic pain in 2023,<sup>[3]</sup> finding treatments that are effective and safe over the long term is essential. The guidelines share three ideas:

- **Combining** several therapies often works better than relying on a single pill.
- **Movement is treatment:** exercise shows up as an option for almost every condition.
- **The mind matters too:** psychological therapy and mindfulness are part of the recommendations for chronic low back pain and fibromyalgia.<sup>[1]</sup>

## If you already take opioids

Don't stop or change your medication on your own. The CDC guideline stresses that any change should be individualized and agreed on with your clinician, avoiding abrupt discontinuation.<sup>[1]</sup>

> A well-designed pain management plan combines diagnosis, movement, emotional support and, when needed, procedures or medications suited to your case.
