**Myofascial pain syndrome** is a chronic condition that affects the muscles and the **fascia**, the thin connective tissue that wraps around them. Its hallmark is the **trigger point**: a tender knot in a tight band of muscle that hurts when pressed and can send pain to another area, known as **referred pain**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Cleveland Clinic notes that experts estimate up to **85%** of people will develop it at some point in their lives.<sup>[1]</sup> The good news: myofascial pain often goes away with treatment.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Where it shows up and how it feels

It is common in the **low back, neck, shoulders and chest**.<sup>[2]</sup> Typical symptoms include:<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

- Aching, throbbing, burning, stinging or stabbing pain, either sudden or a constant dull ache
- **Tender spots** that hurt more when pressed
- A hard or swollen area in the muscle, the "knot" or **taut band**
- Pain that is felt away from the trigger point
- Stiffness, reduced range of motion and a feeling of weakness in the muscle

It often comes with **tension headaches, poor sleep, fatigue, stress, anxiety or depression**.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## Causes and risk factors

The exact cause isn't known. It may start after a **strain or injury** to muscles, ligaments or tendons, or from **overuse**, such as repeating the same movement again and again, or from poor posture.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Cleveland Clinic lists several risk factors: muscle weakness, low activity (for example, after immobilization), cold environments, emotional stress, pinched nerves, thyroid disease, diabetic neuropathy, low vitamin D or folate, and chronic infections.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How is it different from fibromyalgia?

Both cause muscle pain, but they are different conditions. Myofascial pain usually affects **one area**, or several areas on the same side of the body. Fibromyalgia causes **widespread pain** and is more often linked to more severe fatigue, sleep problems, headaches, irritable bowel syndrome and sensations like burning or tingling.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

There is no test that confirms it, and there are no visible signs such as redness or swelling. The diagnosis is made with a **physical exam**: the provider feels for taut bands and tender spots, and pressing a trigger point reproduces the pain. Tests may be ordered to rule out other conditions.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Because the symptoms overlap with other problems, it is often underdiagnosed.<sup>[1]</sup>

## What you can do at home

Ice or heat, regular exercise (stretching, strengthening and aerobic activity), massage, warm soaks and relaxation practices such as yoga or meditation can help.<sup>[1]</sup> Adjusting your workstation, posture and sleep position can reduce muscle tension.<sup>[2]</sup> If you use over-the-counter pain relievers, follow the label and don't combine them with prescription pain relievers or other NSAIDs without advice.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

See a provider if pain in a specific area doesn't go away, ideally **before trigger points develop**: early treatment tends to work better. Physiatrists, pain management specialists, rheumatologists, orthopedists and physical therapists treat this condition.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If a knot in your neck, shoulder or back keeps hurting despite stretching and rest, an assessment can locate the trigger points and decide whether physical therapy, dry needling or an injection could help.
