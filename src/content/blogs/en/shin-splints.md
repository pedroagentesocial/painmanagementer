You started running again, or added more miles in a hurry, and now the **front of your lower leg hurts** during and after every workout. That is typical of **shin splints**, a common exercise-related problem.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> Its medical name is **medial tibial stress syndrome**: inflammation of the muscles, tendons and bone tissue around the shinbone (tibia), with pain along its **inner edge**, where the muscles attach to the bone.<sup>[2]</sup>

The NHS stresses that it is **not serious** and usually gets better **within a few weeks**.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Why it happens

Shin splints develop when the muscle and the tissue that covers the bone become **overworked by repetitive activity**.<sup>[2]</sup> They often appear after a sudden change in training:

**Runners** have the highest risk, and dancers and military recruits are also frequently diagnosed with it.<sup>[2]</sup>

## How it feels

The main symptom is **pain and tenderness along the shinbone**, sometimes with mild swelling.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup> The pain can be **sharp and razor-like or dull and throbbing**, happens both during and after exercise, and gets worse when you press on the sore spot.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Ruling out other causes

The doctor can usually diagnose shin splints by examining your leg and asking about your symptoms.<sup>[1]</sup> But several conditions can cause similar pain, so if it doesn't improve with treatment, other tests may be needed:<sup>[2]</sup>

- **Stress fracture:** one or more small cracks in the tibia caused by stress and overuse; a bone scan or MRI often shows them.
- **Tendinitis:** inflamed tendons, which an MRI can help diagnose.
- **Chronic exertional compartment syndrome:** an uncommon condition in which pressure builds up inside the leg muscles during exercise; the pain usually goes away soon after stopping.

If symptoms don't get better, physical therapy may be needed.<sup>[1]</sup>

## Going back to exercise without relapsing

The AAOS recommends being **pain-free for at least 2 weeks** before returning to exercise, and then not training as often, as long or as hard as before at first.<sup>[2]</sup> Other tips from both organizations:<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

- Don't rush back to your previous level; **build up slowly**.
- Warm up before exercise and stretch afterward.
- Run on **soft ground** when you can, and use shoes designed for your sport that support your feet.
- **Cross-train**, alternating running with lower-impact sports such as swimming or cycling.
- If the same pain returns, **stop**, use ice and rest for a day or two, then restart with shorter, easier sessions and more rest between them.

## When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if you have tried self-care and the pain is getting worse or not getting better, and getting urgent advice if the pain is **severe** or you have **injured your shin**.<sup>[1]</sup> The AAOS adds that if shin splints don't improve with rest and the other measures, a doctor should check whether something else is causing the pain.<sup>[2]</sup>

> If your shins still hurt weeks after easing off training, an assessment can rule out a stress fracture or another cause and help you plan a safe return to exercise.
