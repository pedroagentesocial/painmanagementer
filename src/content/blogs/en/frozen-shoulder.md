At first it is a dull ache when you reach for a shelf or put on a jacket. Weeks later you can barely lift your arm, and the pain wakes you up at night. This is how **frozen shoulder** often feels: the shoulder becomes **painful and stiff for months, sometimes years**.<sup>[1]</sup> Its medical name is **adhesive capsulitis**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What happens inside the shoulder

The shoulder joint is wrapped in strong connective tissue called the **capsule**. In frozen shoulder, this capsule **thickens and becomes stiff and tight**, thick bands of tissue called adhesions form, and there is often less of the fluid that lubricates the joint.<sup>[2]</sup> The NHS explains that the tissue around the joint becomes inflamed, then tightens and shrinks, which causes pain.<sup>[1]</sup>

The typical sign is that the shoulder **can't move well either on its own or when someone else moves it**. That helps tell it apart from other shoulder problems.<sup>[2]</sup>

The good news is that the pain and stiffness **usually go away eventually**.<sup>[1]</sup> According to the AAOS, frozen shoulder generally gets better over time without surgery, although it may take **up to 3 years**.<sup>[2]</sup>

## Who is at higher risk

- **Age and sex:** it most often affects people between **40 and 60**, and women more often than men.<sup>[2]</sup>
- **Diabetes:** frozen shoulder is much more common in people with diabetes, and their stiffness tends to be greater and last longer.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>
- **Other conditions:** thyroid problems (both an underactive and an overactive thyroid), Parkinson's disease and heart disease.<sup>[2]</sup>
- **Not moving the arm:** after an injury, a fracture or surgery that keeps the shoulder still for a while.<sup>[1]</sup><sup>[2]</sup>

The pain is usually **dull or aching**, felt over the outer shoulder and sometimes the upper arm, and it tends to be worse early on and when you move the arm.<sup>[2]</sup> The NHS notes that it can be worse at night and disturb sleep.<sup>[1]</sup>

## How it is diagnosed

The doctor moves your shoulder carefully in all directions and compares that with how far you can move it on your own. X-rays can rule out other problems such as arthritis. An MRI or ultrasound is **not required** to diagnose frozen shoulder, but it may help find other injuries, such as a rotator cuff tear.<sup>[2]</sup>

## What you can do at home

The NHS recommends following the exercises your doctor or physical therapist gives you and **keeping the shoulder moving**, because keeping it still makes the pain worse. It also suggests taking acetaminophen regularly up to the recommended daily dose, and putting a wrapped heat pack on the shoulder for **up to 20 minutes** at a time. Avoid making up your own strenuous exercises, such as using gym equipment, which can make the pain worse.<sup>[1]</sup>

If you have diabetes, the AAOS points out that working with your doctor to improve your blood sugar control can actually help the shoulder recover faster.<sup>[2]</sup>

Stronger painkillers are usually used only for a short time because they can cause side effects.<sup>[1]</sup> Surgery is considered when therapy and other measures haven't helped and is usually offered during the "frozen" stage. After surgery, physical therapy is needed to keep the motion gained, and recovery takes **6 weeks to 3 months**. Long-term results are generally good, although some stiffness can remain, especially in people with diabetes.<sup>[2]</sup>

## When to see a doctor

The NHS advises seeing a doctor if you have shoulder pain and stiffness that doesn't go away, or if the pain is so bad that it is hard to move your arm and shoulder.<sup>[1]</sup>

> If your shoulder has been stiff and painful for weeks and simple measures aren't enough, an assessment can confirm what is going on and help you control the pain while it recovers.
